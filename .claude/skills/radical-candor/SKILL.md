# Radical Candor

A skill for applying the *Radical Candor* framework (Kim Scott) to real
situations: giving guidance, receiving it, and building relationships where
honest communication is normal.

## The core idea

Radical Candor sits at the intersection of two behaviors that must happen
**at the same time**:

1. **Care Personally** — give a damn about the other person as a human being,
   not just their output. Feedback lands only when the receiver believes you
   are on their side.
2. **Challenge Directly** — say the hard thing clearly and promptly. Being
   unclear to spare someone's feelings is not kindness; it denies them the
   information they need to improve.

Dropping either axis produces a failure mode. Use this 2×2 to diagnose any
feedback situation (your own drafts included):

| | Challenge Directly | Avoid Challenging |
|---|---|---|
| **Care Personally** | **Radical Candor** — honest, specific, kind | **Ruinous Empathy** — vague niceness that hides the problem (the most common failure) |
| **Don't Care** | **Obnoxious Aggression** — brutal honesty, criticism as attack | **Manipulative Insincerity** — false praise, political non-answers, backstabbing |

Important framing: these quadrants describe **behaviors, not personality
types**. Never label a *person* "obnoxiously aggressive" — diagnose a specific
piece of guidance. Everyone operates in all four quadrants on different days.

## Order of operations

When helping the user build a candid culture, the sequence matters:

1. **Get feedback first.** Ask for criticism before giving it — you earn the
   right to be candid by proving you can take it. Embrace the discomfort
   (count to six after asking; don't let them off the hook), reward the candor
   visibly, and fix what you can.
2. **Give guidance second.** Praise and criticism, following the rules below.
3. **Encourage it between others third.** Don't be the feedback middleman —
   push colleagues to talk to each other directly.

## Rules for giving guidance

When drafting or coaching feedback, make it:

- **Humble** — state observations and their impact, not verdicts about the
  person. "This report has errors in the totals" not "you're sloppy."
  Offer your view as a view; be open to being wrong.
- **Helpful** — the goal is their success, not venting or being right. Every
  criticism should point toward what better looks like.
- **Immediate** — deliver it in the moment or as close as possible. Saving
  feedback for a review cycle makes small problems big and feels like an
  ambush.
- **In person (or the closest channel available)** — tone travels badly in
  text. For anything sensitive, recommend a call over an email; if it must be
  written, make warmth explicit and unmistakable.
- **Private criticism, public praise** — criticize in private; praise in
  public (unless the person hates public attention). Never criticize someone
  in front of others "to make an example."
- **Not about personality** — target the behavior and the situation, never
  traits, character, or intrinsic ability.

**Praise matters as much as criticism** — and needs the same specificity.
"Great job" is nearly useless; "the way you restructured the intro made the
argument land — do more of that" changes behavior. Aim for a ratio where
praise is frequent, sincere, and specific — but never invent praise as a
spoonful of sugar (that's Manipulative Insincerity, and it teaches people to
brace when they hear a compliment).

**Avoid the feedback sandwich.** Burying criticism between compliments
muddies both. Instead, be caring AND direct in the same breath: "I'm telling
you this because I know you can fix it: X isn't working, here's what I saw."

## Rewriting drafts (common request)

When the user shows you a message they plan to send:

1. Diagnose which quadrant it currently sits in, and say so plainly.
2. Identify what's missing — usually the direct statement of the problem
   (Ruinous Empathy) or the evidence of care (Obnoxious Aggression).
3. Rewrite it to state: the specific situation, the observed behavior, the
   impact, and an invitation to discuss ("here's what I noticed — what's your
   read?"). Keep the user's voice; don't corporate-ify it.
4. If the underlying message is truly severe (firing, formal warning), be
   direct about that too — candor with the user is part of the skill.

## When the user is on the receiving end

- Listen with the intent to understand, not to defend. Look for the kernel of
  truth even in badly delivered criticism.
- Reward the candor — thank them, act on it visibly, or (if you disagree)
  explain your reasoning respectfully. Punishing honesty guarantees silence.
- If a message the user received feels like Obnoxious Aggression, help them
  separate the useful content from the hurtful delivery before responding.
